Reading Response #001: In Plato's Cave

In the opening of On Photography, Susan Sontag writes that “to collect photographs is to collect the world”. This particular line stood out to me, as it feels similar to how I approach using my camera. My camera roll is filled with the little things—whether it's a trinket on my desk, or photos of art on the street. Altogether, these photos form a larger idea of how I view the world. I truly connect with Sontag when she describes photographs as a way of claiming something; there is some level of importance in the photos I take. with that said, a point of concern for me when taking a photo is not being fully present/being too concerned with recording or having a momento instead. This relates to how Sontag points out that photography is “a way of imprisoning reality”. While I do worry about not living in the moment, I also know that without the photo, the memory fades faster. So for me, the image becomes both proof and limitation; it keeps the memory alive, whilst becoming the memory, sometimes even replacing the actual experience.

Another part of Sontag's essay I connect with is when she writes about how photography is both social and personal. I see this in how people share images—thus parts of themselves, online. Taking photos goes beyond showcasing parts of our lives on the internet. Sometimes, it's simply about having something to hold onto. With the age of the internet, we often hear the phrase, "the internet is forever", which usually has a negative connotation. However, the awareness that comes with posting on the internet is now widespread, and this phrase can now be interpreted as a mindset of how people share online. Since "the internet is forever", couldn't it be a place to document memories and certain parts of our lives eternally? Though a majority of the photos I take may not end up on social media, that does not mean it's on the "cloud", or backed up somewhere on a hard drive. The ones I do post are often carefully curated, and add onto a image I had created for myself online; there's a split there—a line between public image and personal memories.

With the projects we have in this course, I want to highlight the contrast the internet has brought to our generation. Though its purpose is to bring people together and help build connections, it is hard to deny that the internet, specifically social media, has made us feel more disconnected than ever. Through my work, I want to spotlight how the internt/online culture has simultaneously made connection easier than ever, yet brings a sense of disconnect to the younger generations. We share so much of ourselves online, but just how much of that is genuine? How much of it is curated? Pairing images with text could highlight this; an image/photography could capture the non-curated aspect, while illustration or text could bring out the parts of ourselves we curate. What I take away from this reading is that every photo—every image has some kind of weight to it. There is something to say about the world we live in. Whether its "junk" photos, or images from a photoshoot, I want to be more intentional with the photos I take and the art I create.



Reading Response #002: AI Slop

Nesrine Malik’s piece, “With ‘AI slop’ distorting our reality, the world is sleepwalking into disaster,” reads less like a tech rant and more like a cultural diagnosis. She uses the term “AI slop” to describe the flood of low-effort, AI-generated content reshaping how we see and share things online. These fake but believable images fill our feeds until the line between real and fabricated quietly fades. Malik’s tone isn’t alarmist—it’s observant and uneasy. She’s not afraid of technology itself, but of what happens when we stop noticing how it shapes us. What stands out most is her claim that AI slop isn’t neutral—it’s built on bias. Since AI is trained on existing data, it tends to repeat old stereotypes about gender, race, and “traditional” values. Malik argues this gives AI-generated content a conservative slant. It doesn’t create new perspectives; it reinforces what’s already familiar. I found that point especially sharp because it shows how technology, even when it feels new, often mirrors the same hierarchies that exist offline.

Malik also focuses on scale and subtlety—two things that make this problem hard to see. Most AI slop isn’t openly harmful; it can be funny, aesthetic, or emotionally manipulative in small ways. But the sheer amount of it dulls our sense of what’s real. We may not believe every image we see, but we stop questioning them altogether. That feels especially relevant for people who spend most of their social time online. Our perception of “realness” becomes something the algorithm decides for us. Malik’s point about private sharing hit home. She writes about relatives who forward AI-generated images on WhatsApp, believing them because they trust the sender. In these closed spaces, there’s no room for correction or context—just quiet reinforcement. I’ve seen this happen in my own family, where AI-edited photos or fake headlines circulate as truth. Malik captures how misinformation spreads subtly, not through big conspiracies but through everyday familiarity.

Her essay also made me think about my own habits. How often do I stop to check if something is real—or do I just accept it because it looks plausible? Malik’s image of society “sleepwalking” feels accurate. The danger isn’t dramatic; it’s quiet, steady, and normalized. We’re surrounded by digital noise that feels harmless until it replaces our sense of truth. If her piece leaves anything unanswered, it’s what comes next. Malik mentions regulation and education but offers no fixed solutions. Maybe that’s intentional—the real goal is to make us pay attention. Her essay lingers as a reminder that AI’s biggest threat isn’t its capability, but our willingness to let it think for us.

Reading Response #003: Motherhood in Crisis

For this response, I chose Rosa's story; the mother navigating postpartum depression alone during the early phase of the pandemic. What struck me was the decision to pair her narrative not only with photographs of he daily environment, but also with soft, nearly fragile illustrations hovering between memory and emotion. The illustrations don't try to replicate reality. Rather, they hold the parts of Rosas experience that are harder to photograph—loneliness, fear, the heaviness of routine, and the way these feelings weigh on the body. Visually, they feel more interior. The phootgraphs show us concrete details: the room she's in, the light on the floor, the messiness that accumulates when someone is just trying to survive the day. The illustrations, in contrast, show the emotional truth that isn't necessarily available to the camera. The lines are much looser, more interpretive. Sometimes they blur or dissolve entirely, mirroring how Rosa describes losing her sense of self in the midst of caregiving and isolation. This visual tension—between what’s literally there and what’s emotionally present—makes the story more accessible. A photograph insists on a certain kind of reality, and while that can be powerful, it can also be limiting. Trauma, exhaustion, or the quiet unraveling that happens in private moments is not always visible. Illustration opens a door for the viewer to inhabit those invisible moments. It creates an atmosphere instead of an image. In Rosa’s story, the illustration of her holding her baby with her own figure partially faded and hollowed out communicates more about her internal state than any photo could. It’s not about accuracy; it’s about resonance.

Illustrations also offer a safe space for stories that carry vulnerability or stigma. Postpartum depression is deeply personal and still misunderstood in many contexts. Using illustration allows the story to maintain intimacy without exposing the individual to the same level of surveillance we associate with documentary photography. It respects the mother’s privacy while still inviting the audience to feel the weight of her experience. This is something text alone struggles to do. Text explains, but illustration allows viewers to sense. It gives form to emotional experiences that are hard to articulate. Compared to text-only storytelling, illustration slows the reader down. It signals moments of emotional importance—places where we should pause, breathe, and take in what’s happening beyond the factual narrative. And compared to photography, illustration can exaggerate, distort, or abstract in ways that deepen our connection rather than distance us. Photography can sometimes create a barrier; we look at someone else’s life from the outside. Illustration brings us inside. It encourages interpretation, empathy, and imagination.

In a collection built from text, illustration, photography, and video, each medium fills a gap the others can’t. Rosa’s story demonstrates why illustration matters: it protects, reveals, and transforms. It reminds us that motherhood during crisis is not only something seen but something deeply felt—and that feeling requires a visual language flexible enough to hold it.

Reading Response #004: The Rising Risk, and How We Stop It

The New York Times interactive on nuclear war prevention felt personal due to how its visuals carried the weight of the argument; this was in contrast to what I was initially expecting, which was a piece that would be more dense and policy-heavy. Instead of being met with paragraphs of geopolitical analysis, I found myself pulled in by the pacing of the graphics—slow, deliberate, almost quiet. The design makes you sit with the information rather than skim over it. For a subject as overwhelming as nuclear war, that shift in mood matters. It made me realize that the piece wasn’t just trying to inform the reader, but guide them emotionally through something that is usually too abstract to fully grasp. What the visuals do especially well here is give shape to scale. Reading numbers about stockpiles or treaties creates a certain distance; you understand the facts, but not necessarily the stakes. Seeing those numbers placed on a world map or animated across decades forces a different kind of recognition. Suddenly, the threat isn’t “somewhere else” or “just history.” It becomes present. I felt that most clearly when the interactive showed nuclear arsenals rising and falling over time. Even though it was just a simple shifting graphic, the movement made the instability feel real. There’s something unsettling about watching years pass and noticing how little the danger has actually disappeared.

The piece also uses visuals to communicate the unevenness of nuclear risk. Some countries hold enormous arsenals; others hold only a few. But the scale doesn’t make anyone safer—if anything, it complicates the global landscape. A static article could explain this, but the visual comparison makes it intuitive. You don’t have to decode the argument; you just see it. Another strength of the interactive is how it handles imagination. Nuclear war is so massive that words often fail. It’s difficult to picture destruction at that scale without leaning into fear or sensationalism. The interactive avoids that by using clean, simple diagrams to model blast radiuses, fallout patterns, and decision timelines. It’s factual but intimate. The visuals give you just enough form to understand the consequences, while still respecting the emotional weight of what is being shown. What surprised me most is how the visuals create accessibility. When dealing with something as heavy as nuclear policy, text alone can feel distant or too technical. The graphics break the argument into moments—small, focused pieces you can digest without feeling overwhelmed. They create breathing room. I found myself reading more attentively because the visuals structured the experience.

Overall, the interactive made a topic I usually think of as distant feel immediate and more engaging (especially given the current political climate). The combination of visuals and text created clarity without losing emotional depth. It reminded me that nuclear danger isn’t just information, but also something we have to feel the urgency of in order to understand why prevention matters. The interactive’s visual storytelling makes that urgency visible. If anything, it proves that sometimes seeing the argument is what finally makes it real.
